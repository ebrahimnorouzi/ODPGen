Scenario:
As a representative real-world environment, we take the LyonTech La Doua campus as a navigable environment. A subpart of this campus is illustrated in Figure 1, which contrasts two itineraries connecting points A and B. The shortest route (shown in blue) leaves the starting building directly, follows a tram line and arrives from the west. Another possible route (orange line), which may prove more convenient depending on the user’s preferences, passes through several indoor areas and arrives from the east. Although shorter in distance, the blue itinerary exposes the user to outdoor weather, tramway noise, and a potentially non-accessible entrance, which may pose difficulties to some users. The orange itinerary maximizes indoor traversal, avoids noise, and reaches point B through a more accessible side, which are environmental characteristics that may be looked after by some user. Identifying such an itinerary therefore requires accounting for contextual characteristics as well as user preferences and constraints, including sensory factors (e.g., noise level), physical accessibility (e.g., entrance type, vertical transitions), and dynamic conditions (e.g., weather, lighting). In this section, we describe how we made sure to build an inclusive-by-design ontology. To this end, we collaborated with a domain expert with a background in occupational therapy, who contributed to the elaboration of a reference vocabulary cataloging environmental characteristics considered as potentially relevant to users in pedestrian mobility. Building on this work, we derived several personas, two of which are presented below. The first persona, Anna, is a first-year undergraduate student in Computer Science diagnosed with autism spectrum disorder. She feels uncomfortable in crowded or noisy places and needs opportunities to rest in quiet spaces. Based on Anna’s profile, we derived the following requirements: R1 The routing system should characterize spatial areas through relevant contextual properties (e.g. noise level, crowdedness). R2 The system should identify and help to avoid high-traffic areas during peak hours. R3 The system should be able to locate rest areas along or near to potential routes. The second persona, Sacha, is a master’s student in Sports Science who prefers physically engaging routes (stairs over elevators, outdoor green spaces) and occasionally needs quick access to specific amenities. Her profile motivates the following requirements: R4 The system should distinguish between vertical transition types (stairs, elevators, escalators) to support preference and capability-aware routing. R5 The system should locate the nearest instance of a desired amenity type (vending machine, toilets, reception board) to the current proposed itinerary. R6 The system should characterize spaces based on their aesthetic or comfort-related qualities to enable experience-aware routing.
